## Creative brief

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

To understand how a novel pathogen spilling out of the Amazon might spark the next pandemic, Reuters used an epidemiological model to simulate the spread of a virus even more contagious than COVID-19 – a scenario scientists say could easily happen. I was tasked with coming up with a visualisation that shows the phenomenon and its global impact using the narration of the hypothetical virus.

</div>
</Container>

<Container grid style="margin-block-start:var(--space-xl)">
<div class="hed col-span-md-5">

## The simulation dataset

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

The simulation, modelled by the data team using <LinkButton label='GleamViz'  url='https://www.gleamviz.org/' /> – a simulator for epidemiological modelling, produced two simple datasets --

- <p>Seedings: a list of source and target cities that the virus spread to from day 0 to day 180.</p>
- <p>Infections: cases estimated in every city and daily global totals.</p>

If you are curious about how the data was generated by the simulation, you can read more about it <LinkButton label='here'  url='https://www.reuters.com/investigates/special-report/global-pandemic-bats-methodology/' />.

</div>
</Container>

## Understanding the data

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

Since the data included geographic coordinates, my first response was to load it up in QGIS–a geospatial analysis tool– to see what the spread of the cities looked like. It was evident that there was quite an overlap of cities—the same city turned into a transmitter once it was infected with the virus.

</div>
<div class="hed col-span-md-5">

### What does the transmission look like?

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

I split the seedings into source and destination datasets and ran them through the hub-spoke algorithm in QGIS to generate virus “flight paths.” Animating those paths with the QGIS Temporal Controller tool gave me an overview of what was happening over a period of 180 days.

</div>
</Container>

<Container width="lg">

<OverflowImage breakpoint=480 maxWidth='150%' shadow="{true}" url='media/projects/bats-pandemic/fig_3.gif' alt="Animated QGIS map of virus seedings over time, starting in Brazil and spreading across the world." caption="Animated view of the virus seedings in QGIS. It is evident that the transmission starts slowly in Brazil and then speeds up as it spreads all over the world." />

<OverflowImage breakpoint=480 maxWidth='150%' shadow="{true}" url='media/projects/bats-pandemic/fig_4.webp' alt="Static map of all seedings at the end of the 180 day period, with dense clusters of lines around major cities." caption="All seedings at the end of the 180 day period. It highlights that some cities (like national capitals, and tourist destinations) are hotspots of transmission." />

</Container>

<Container grid style="margin-block-start:var(--space-xl)">
<div class="col-start-md-6 col-span-md-7">

Now that I had an overview of the data, I wanted to delve into the numbers for insights into the dynamics of the hypothetical spread. I loaded it up in an <LinkButton label='Observable Notebook'  url='https://observablehq.com/' /> —my tool of choice for data analysis beyond Microsoft Excel. I can use javascript array functions or d3-array methods to quickly transform and understand the data and then visualise insights using the Plot library.

The result, shown below, was a connected dot plot that served as a timeline for the spread. Each dot represents a city, and is coloured by country. The y-axis is the time scale—the number of days since the original outbreak. (Note that the lower values on the y-axis are early days, so the only dots are in grey, the colour of Brazil.) The x-axis is the number ID assigned to the cities for easy mapping of source and targets.

</div>
</Container>

<Container width="lg">

<OverflowImage breakpoint=720 maxWidth='180%' shadow="{false}" url='media/projects/bats-pandemic/fig_5.webp' alt="Connected dot plot with cities on the x-axis and days on the y-axis, dots coloured by country and lines showing transmissions." caption="A connected dot plot with cities (identified by a numeric ID) on the x-axis and days on the y-axis. Dots show the target city, coloured by country. A line shows a transmission on a particular day. This view helped visually narrow down when transmissions went international (longer lines) from Brazil (around day 60)." />

</Container>

## Ideating the narrative and the visuals

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

The primary aspect of the data was how the virus spreads over time and space. By focusing on these variables in the data, I drafted an outline, as follows:

- The virus started from Altamira, Brazil and got transmitted locally
- The virus broke free and moved to the US
- Eventually, it spread to Europe and Asia
- Cities emerged as viral hotspots
- Nearly the entire world is affected by the hypothetical pandemic

</div>
<div class="hed col-span-md-5">

### Secondary research

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

I looked at visualisations from Gleamviz and other sources for inspiration around epidemic visualisation. I found many geo-visualisations or network diagrams that show viral propagation.

</div>
</Container>

### Visual explorations and prototyping

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

I plotted the seedings using a d3-graph on Observable. One thing this graph doesn’t show is geography, which makes it hard to demonstrate some of the points in the narrative. It looks good though, as it visually resonates with the form of a virus.

</div>
</Container>

<Container width="lg">

<OverflowImage breakpoint=720 maxWidth='180%' shadow="{false}" url='media/projects/bats-pandemic/fig_9.webp' alt="Seedings drawn as force-directed network graphs that resemble virus particles." caption="Seedings as force-directed graphs." />

</Container>

<Container grid style="margin-block-start:var(--space-xl)">
<div class="col-start-md-6 col-span-md-7">

Placing the seedings on a globe solved the geography problem. A globe is a familiar form and carries a lot of impact for a story about something that touches the entire world, so I drew the infections as circles over the cities.

</div>
</Container>

## Building the globe visualisation

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

I built the chart as a standalone module that could be dropped into the story and driven by the narrative. It needed a long list of features.

</div>
</Container>

<Container width="lg">

<OverflowImage breakpoint=720 maxWidth='180%' shadow="{false}" url='media/projects/bats-pandemic/fig_12.webp' alt="Eight sticky notes listing the planned features: animated seeding lines, local and international transmission, lofted flight paths, pulsing beacons, infection symbols, text labels, globe design effects, and fly to location, spin, drag and zoom." caption="The feature list for the globe chart." />

</Container>

<Container grid style="margin-block-start:var(--space-xl)">
<div class="col-start-md-6 col-span-md-7">

A flat circle does not look like a globe, so I used layers of light and shadow gradients to give it depth and a 3D feel.

</div>
</Container>

<Container width="lg">

<OverflowImage breakpoint=480 maxWidth='150%' shadow="{false}" url='media/projects/bats-pandemic/fig_13.webp' alt="A globe built from layered light and shadow gradients." caption="The globe, built from layered light and shadow gradients." />

</Container>

<Container grid style="margin-block-start:var(--space-xl)">
<div class="col-start-md-6 col-span-md-7">

Rendering the 2,000+ animated lines on a canvas dropped the frame rate below 10 FPS. Instead of learning three.js from scratch, I moved the drawing to PIXI.js, a WebGL renderer, and kept d3 for the projections and the data. The chart became a stack of layers, each rendered on its own.

</div>
</Container>

<Container width="lg">

<div class="hed col-span-md-5">

## Designing for the story

</div>
<div class="dek col-span-md-7">

The chart module could do a lot, but the story needed only some of it. As the art direction moved to dark mode, I settled on a grayscale palette with red as the single spot colour, and scaled back the exploratory features so the visualisation would serve the narration. The final piece is a scrollytelling experience built with Svelte and tuned to run smoothly on laptops.

</div>
